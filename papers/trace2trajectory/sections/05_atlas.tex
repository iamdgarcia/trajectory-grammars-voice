% §5 The trajectory atlas
The trajectory atlas renders the coverage computation as one behavior plane: real walks as
blue dots, simulated walks as orange diamonds, uncovered cells shaded red with
nearest-exemplar tooltips, outcome classes readable by position (and by per-class views as
small multiples).
It is simultaneously the paper's worked example and the artifact's operational interface.
Figure~\ref{fig:atlas} shows the schema.

\subsection{Reading the atlas}
Four configurations recur.
\emph{Collapse}: the sim cloud concentrates on one real lobe (the over-cooperative user who
never abandons, never escalates --- cf.\ the Formalism Ceiling \citep{realUserSim2026} and the
human-likeness failures of \citep{mirrorbench2026}); coverage is high on the majority class
and near zero elsewhere.
\emph{Scatter}: the sim cloud spreads where real conversations never go --- off-distribution
generation that inflates the plane without measuring anything; the shared threshold of
Equation~\eqref{eq:threshold} keeps these points from manufacturing coverage, and
out-of-grid points are dropped rather than clamped.
\emph{Coverage with holes}: the sim cloud overlaps real mass but misses specific occupied
cells --- the most actionable configuration, because the holes come with named exemplars.
\emph{Blur masking} (Section~\ref{sec:coverage}): at wide kernels the sim cloud's mass
reaches cells it does not occupy, painting interior holes as covered; a red-free atlas at
wide bandwidth is not evidence of reach --- the instance number and the low-$\beta$ rungs
carry that verdict.
Cross-model comparison is legitimate by design: real logs and simulated runs may come from
different vendors and model generations, and the representation comparison rests on
cross-model convergence of layer representations \citep{layersSimilarDepths2025} and the
trajectory-geometry vocabulary \citep{trajectoryGeometry2026}.

\subsection{Worked example on shipped fixtures}
The open-source artifact ships fixtures in three dialects and regenerates every number below
from a single command.
The worked example uses 3 conversations across 3 dialects --- two real (one OTel GenAI export,
one OpenInference export) and one simulated (a LangSmith export) --- under the grammar feature
encoding, a $24 \times 24$ grid, $\varepsilon = 0.05$, bandwidth $\beta = 0.12$.
The real set occupies 256 grid cells; the 1-conversation simulated set covers 103 of them.

\subsection{Extraction on third-party data}
\label{sec:multiwoz}
Nothing in $\tau$ is tuned to the artifact's fixtures, and the mapping is defined on any
structured dialogue stream --- so it must run, verbatim and untuned, on a public benchmark of
conversations nobody in this paper recorded.
The MultiWOZ 2.2 test split \citep{multiwoz2026,multiwoz2018} (1{,}000 human--human task-oriented dialogues,
five primary domains: restaurant, attraction, train, taxi, hotel) is converted to the
transcript dialect and mapped by the same \texttt{infer\_states()} used for the observability
dialects, with the benchmark's own \texttt{services} annotation as the task label
(Definition~\ref{def:instance} conditioning).
Outcome decidability carries over: contained requires an explicit assistant closing phrase,
abandoned is its absence at stream end.

The result separates what belongs to the mapper from what belongs to the corpus.
Extraction is total (1{,}000/1{,}000 dialogues mapped, no errors) and the per-domain close
rates differ (restaurant 56.7\% vs.\ train 48.8\%): the domain label induces a distribution
the mapper did not put there.
The two zeros are corpus properties, not mapper failures, and they are themselves coverage
statements: MultiWOZ contains no handoff concept and never exercises the clarify-reopen loop
structure, so $D=0$ and handoff$=0$ across all five domains.
A consistency check stress-tested exactly this claim: a generous dialogue-universal net over
the test split (\emph{sorry?}, \emph{pardon}, \emph{say that again}, \emph{didn't catch},
\emph{one more time}) finds 5/1{,}000 dialogues with a repeat-style request, and all five are
task retries (\emph{check again please} = re-run a search), not user-side clarify metaprompts
--- the corpus genuinely never re-opens a settled state.
A benchmark that never exercises a behavior cannot select simulators that produce it --- the
same statement the atlas makes about simulator corpora, made here about a benchmark.

\subsubsection*{Schema-Guided Dialogue: verdict vs.\ structural truth}
The Schema-Guided Dialogue test split \citep{sgd2019,sgd2020} (4{,}201 human--human dialogues
across 20 services) carries what MultiWOZ does not: a native record of every service call and
its results, so the terminal-outcome verdict can be confronted with structural truth rather
than only with domain labels.
Extraction is again total (4{,}201/4{,}201 mapped verbatim, no errors), the wizard signature
holds for a third corpus (handoff$=0$ and $D=0$ across all 20 services, structural abandonment
9/4{,}201 $=0.2\%$), and per-service close rates separate (58.8\% to 88.2\%).

The confrontation separates two constructs that the close rate conflates.
Exact agreement between the walker's verdict and service-call truth is 55.3\% verbatim.
The largest share of the gap is close vocabulary: the shipped closing phrases are coupled to
the corpora the artifact was built from, and about 22 percentage points of the disagreement
are real assistant closings (\emph{have a wonderful day}, \emph{enjoy your concert}) the
untuned tables do not recognize; recalibrating that vocabulary on the benchmark's own
\emph{dev} split (2{,}482 dialogues; the test split is never used for calibration) and
re-measuring on \emph{test} raises exact agreement to 83.2\%.
The remainder is behavioral, not lexical: mid-conversation closings followed by re-entry, and
closings with no delivered result.

The second residue is the more interesting measurement.
Five dialogues ($0.12\%$) end with a service call that formally succeeds but returns zero
results, an assistant that confirms the failure aloud, and a polite closing the walker reads
as contained; a further 107 of 2{,}326 contained verdicts ($4.6\%$) carry the same
tool-ok/empty-result signal as candidates.
This is the confident-closing silent-failure mode measured in third-party data: conversational
closure is observable from the transcript, delivered outcome is not, and a politeness signal
is evidence of neither.
Verdict and truth measure different things, and the distance between them, which this
benchmark pins exactly, is the finding: any outcome accounting built from transcripts alone
silently absorbs it.
\label{sec:sgd}

\subsubsection*{tau-bench: anchoring the atlas on real agent runs}
\label{sec:taubench}
Wizard-of-oz corpora verify the extraction against human conversations; they cannot anchor
the atlas on agent behavior, because they contain none.
The official \texttt{historical\_trajectories} of tau-bench \citep{taubench2024} are exactly
that anchor: 1{,}980 episodes of two production-grade LLM agents (gpt-4o, 660; claude-3.5-sonnet,
1{,}320; airline and retail splits), each with the environment-verified reward as native
outcome truth.
The extraction is total (1{,}980/1{,}980 mapped verbatim, no errors), and the shipped taubench
dialect is internally consistent with its reward injection in 1{,}980/1{,}980 episodes.

Confronting the conversational verdict (terminal event stripped, so the walker decides on
the dialogue alone, as in \S\ref{sec:sgd}) with the environment reward sharpens the
construct separation on real agents:
contained is given for 17.3\% of passing episodes and 21.7\% of failing ones, and a closing
phrase appears in 66.8\% of passing and 76.4\% of failing episodes.
The conversational close does not merely fail to predict the delivered outcome; its sign is
inverted, so the close-rate proxy that separated human domains (Tables~\ref{tab:multiwoz})
does not transfer to agent runs.
A further asymmetry goes the other way: 628 of 1{,}980 real runs ($31.7\%$) re-enter a
settled state ($D$ mean 0.47, max 9), while $D=0$ across all three human corpora.
Real agents exercise the clarify-reopen loop that wizard benchmarks never do; a simulator
selected on wizard corpora cannot be tested for the loop its target agents actually enter.
And the silent-failure family is agent-specific too: the tool-ok/empty-result flag of
\S\ref{sec:sgd} fires zero times here (tau-bench failures are policy violations caught by
write checks, not empty result sets), so that signal captures one family of the mode, not
the mode.

The two right panels of Figure~\ref{fig:atlas-tb} anchor the atlas itself on this real
collection.
The 1{,}980 runs occupy 961 distinct feature positions (the grammar-shaped space is discrete
and dense) and 243 grid cells at the shipped kernel 0.12.
The artifact's 60 production-shaped sim compositions, projected into the same
real-fitted plane, touch only 17 of those cells directly, and none lands exactly on a real
position: instance coverage is 0\% (radius $r=0$, median real NN distance), while kernel
coverage reads 100\% and stays 100\% across the full bandwidth ladder (101/130/243 cells
occupied at 0.02/0.05/0.12).
This is the kernel-masking separation of \S4 reproduced with a real anchor: the wide kernel
claims the simulator set covers everything; the instance metric shows it covers nothing
exactly.
Both statements are data-true; they measure different things, and the atlas reports both.

\subsection{Relation to the simulator program}
The atlas is where calibrated simulation is \emph{validated}.
The calibrated-simulation agenda --- response-level gap closure
\citep{distributionalGap2026}, calibrated user embeddings \citep{cue2026}, grounded user
simulation \citep{realUserSim2026} --- produces better simulators; region coverage is the
instrument that says whether the improvement reached the conversation structures that matter.
The secondary instruments for that validation are Wasserstein divergence of grammar-walk
statistics in the style of the Conversational Distribution Score \citep{cds2026} and the rank
correlation of Section~\ref{sec:evaluation}; region coverage is the headline, because it is
the only one of the three that returns actionable per-region output.
